MWSR (MoonlightWeb Super Resolution) is the real-time upscaler of MoonlightWeb's browser client, open source under the MIT License. Here it and three other single-frame upscalers draw the same picture on your GPU. Each pane shows its output pixel for pixel, next to the native frame it tries to rebuild. The table scores each one against that frame and times it on this device. The README explains the method and gives the figures for two GPUs.

What MWSR is

MWSR is a spatial upscaler: it rebuilds each frame from that frame alone, in one GPU pass, with no motion vectors, no frame history and no neural network, so it waits for no other frame and suits a live stream. It decides once per 2×2 block of pixels: a flat block keeps a smooth cubic, an edge gets four more samples along and across it. Each pixel stays within its four input texels, so nothing rings. It also reduces a stream larger than its screen, averaging in linear light; it reads HDR past 1.0 as it is; and on a 4:4:4 stream it sharpens colour edges too.

MoonlightWeb runs it in the browser, on WebGPU or WebGL2. The mwsr folder gives it to any renderer under the MIT License: WGSL, GLSL ES 3.00 and HLSL texts, and a C++ header for Direct3D 11 and 12, all written from the module the client runs.

MWSR zones is MoonlightWeb's own, for its native host. The host measures each zone of the frame it captures (a 32×18 grid) and MWSR lowers its sharpness where a zone wants less, never above its law; a zone beside one at the law keeps the law, so text next to a photo stays crisp. This page measures the native frame as the host does. It is not in the mwsr folder: the MWSR anyone downloads draws by the law, the MWSR line above.

How to read it

  • The native frame is the picture at 2560×1440. The input is that frame reduced to 1920×1080 or 1280×720, by Lanczos-3 in linear light, the same reduction as the README. Each upscaler draws the input back at 2560×1440.
  • ×1 gives every upscaler the frame itself, to draw at its own size. MoonlightWeb copies such a frame whatever the upscaler; the page runs each one all the same, to show what it does to a picture it does not enlarge.
  • ×0.75 and ×0.5 give the 2560×1440 frame as the input, drawn at 1920×1080 or 1280×720: a stream larger than its screen. The scores are taken against the reference, the frame reduced by the same Lanczos-3 in linear light. MWSR runs its reduction, as the client does: bilinear samples over each pixel's footprint, averaged in linear light (through the textures' sRGB views; on HDR, from the light itself). FSR1, NIS and SGSR1 are made to enlarge (NIS from ×1 to ×2): below ×1 they run as they are, as the client runs them.
  • Fidelity is the SSIM of the luma against the native frame, ×100: the README's measure, computed here on what your GPU drew. Colour is the mean SSIM of the red, green and blue channels, which sees the edges the luma misses: those of the colour text, whose colours all have one luma. On HDR both are taken on the PQ code of each BT.2020 channel, and the overshoot in nits (past 1 % + 0.5 nit), as the lab measures HDR.
  • Overshoot is the share of pixels that leave the range of their four input texels by more than 2/255. It counts halos and ringing.
  • GPU time is the median of batches of 100 draws, each batch timed by two GPU timestamps. The browser rounds those timestamps (to 0.1 ms in Chrome), and a batch of 100 makes the rounding a small share. When your GPU has shader-f16, MWSR (its enlargement), FSR1 and SGSR1 are also timed in 16-bit and the faster of their two precisions is shown. Within 2 % of each other the two are even, f16 ≈ f32: such a gap changes sides from one run to the next. Their 16-bit picture is first held to their 32-bit one: within 4/255 on 99.9 % of the pixels. The pictures and the scores are those of the 32-bit texts. Timings vary with the GPU's clock: run them twice before you trust a small gap.
  • 4:2:0 gives every upscaler the input a 4:2:0 stream would give it: its colour averaged over 2×2 pixels and brought back by interpolation, as a decoder hands it over. This page makes both inputs itself, so every GPU shows them. A 4:4:4 stream needs more: a host whose encoder makes it (on MoonlightWeb's own host, NVENC in H.264 or HEVC) and a browser that decodes it. MoonlightWeb streams it in 8-bit SDR and offers it only where the browser says it decodes it.
  • MWSR sharpens colour edges too runs CHROMA444, the mode MoonlightWeb turns on by itself for a 4:4:4 stream: MWSR then also sharpens the border between two colours of one brightness, as in coloured text or code. Zoom on the colour text to see it. It does nothing on 4:2:0, whose colours are already blurred, and it is off by default, as in the README's figures.
  • HDR: the pictures are the lab's, drawn by this page with 1.0 = 203 nits and highlights up to 1000 nits. MWSR and bilinear read the floats past 1.0 as they are. FSR1 runs inside AMD's reversible tone map (FsrSrtm), as AMD prescribes. NIS and SGSR1 run inside the same tone map, because neither port reads past 1.0 (MoonlightWeb's NIS port has no NIS_HDR_MODE, and SGSR1 clamps to 0..1). The tone map is not timed.
  • Your picture is decoded in this tab, and a picture larger than 3840×2160 is first reduced to fit. As the native frame, it is reduced and rebuilt, and scored (below ×1, it is the input, scored against its own Lanczos-3 reduction). As the input, it is upscaled as it is and only the overshoot is scored. Pictures are read as 8-bit SDR.

The ports

These are MoonlightWeb's ports, the ones the client runs, not the vendors' own builds. FSR1 is firdawolf's WGSL port of EASU and RCAS (RCAS at 0.75 stops), with the lab's port of AMD's 16-bit path (FsrEasuH, FsrRcasH). NIS is a per-fragment transcription of NVScaler (SDK 1.0.3) at sharpness 0.5. NVIDIA ships a compute shader with shared memory, which is faster, so its time here is the port's, not NIS's. SGSR1 is a port of sgsr1.h (mode 1, edge threshold 6/255, edge sharpness 1.5), in 32-bit and in half precision. MWSR runs with its defaults.
