MWSR (MoonlightWeb Super Resolution)

MWSR is the real-time upscaler of MoonlightWeb's browser client, open source under the MIT License. Here it and three other single-frame upscalers draw the same picture on your GPU. Each pane shows its output pixel for pixel, next to the native frame it tries to rebuild. The table gives each one's scores against that frame, its overshoot and its GPU time, all measured on this device when you press its button. The README explains the method and gives the figures for three GPUs.

What MWSR is

MWSR is a spatial upscaler: it rebuilds each frame from that frame alone, in one GPU pass, with no motion vectors, no frame history and no neural network, so it waits for no other frame and suits a live stream. It decides once per 2×2 block of pixels: a flat block keeps a single read, an edge gets a cubic and the detail measured across it. It leaves a little overshoot on purpose, which a person's eye preferred to a harder bound. It also reduces a stream larger than its screen, averaging in linear light and sharpening a little from ×0.5 to 1:1; it reads HDR past 1.0, without that overshoot; and on a 4:4:4 stream it sharpens colour edges too.

MoonlightWeb runs it in the browser, on WebGPU or WebGL2. The mwsr folder gives it to any renderer under the MIT License: WGSL, GLSL ES 3.00 and HLSL texts, and a C++ header for Direct3D 11 and 12, all written from the module the client runs.

Measured on three GPUs

The README's figures: its times from 03/11/2026, the Radeon 780M's from 04/11/2026, NIS's on the RTX and the iGPU from 11/11/2026 (after its port was written out without arrays), in Chrome's WebGPU on Windows 11, the input already in an 8-bit texture, on the 0 A.D. picture (game) and the text chart (text). The scores are the lab's, on SwiftShader at 4:4:4, by the definitions of the table above; the times per draw, MWSR in 32-bit, the text MoonlightWeb runs, the others at their faster precision, marked half when it is half precision (16-bit). The Ryzen 9 9900X's integrated Radeon ran at its own clock: read its times as ratios. The Radeon 780M was held at 938 MHz, memory at 2800 MHz: a bench clock, not what a stream gets, as this GPU moves between 800 and 2800 MHz left to itself. NIS is MoonlightWeb's port (see the ports below). In bold, the best of each score.

On phones (04/11/2026, the whole draw of a frame up to the GPU's end, one pass per case, on clocks nothing fixed): on an iPhone 13 Pro, in Safari and Chrome, MWSR adds 0.5 to 2.1 ms to a frame, upscaling or reducing, where FSR1 adds 2.8 to 5.4 ms. On a Realme with an entry-level Mali-G57, it adds 4 to 5 ms from half the screen's size (19 to 22 ms a frame, FSR1 44 ms) and 12 to 13 ms from 720p to 1440p (35 to 36 ms a frame, FSR1 108 to 115 ms). That does not hold 60 frames a second, and Auto leaves it off on that GPU.

How to read it

  • The native frame is the picture at 2560×1440. The input is that frame reduced to 1920×1080 or 1280×720, by Lanczos-3 in linear light, the same reduction as the README. Each upscaler draws the input back at 2560×1440.
  • ×1 gives every upscaler the frame itself, to draw at its own size. MoonlightWeb copies such a frame whatever the upscaler; the page runs each one all the same, to show what it does to a picture it does not enlarge.
  • ×0.75 and ×0.5 give the 2560×1440 frame as the input, drawn at 1920×1080 or 1280×720: a stream larger than its screen. The scores are taken against the reference, the frame reduced by the same Lanczos-3 in linear light. MWSR runs its reduction, as the client does: bilinear samples over each pixel's footprint, averaged in linear light (through the textures' sRGB views; on HDR, from the light itself), then from ×0.5 to 1:1 a light sharpening where the block sees detail, gentler on a strong edge and none on HDR. FSR1, NIS and SGSR1 are made to enlarge (NIS from ×1 to ×2): below ×1 they run as they are, as the client runs them.
  • The scores are computed in this tab when you press the button, each one as soon as it is ready. They leave it only at the end of a measure. LPIPS (AlexNet, v0.1) says how far a picture looks from the native frame to a human eye: it was made to judge upscalers and image restoration, and lower is closer. Its network runs on your GPU, and the page fetches its 9.9 MB of weights for it. VMAF NEG is Netflix's VMAF in its no-enhancement-gain mode: it judges a video stream's pictures as viewers rate them, without crediting the added sharpening that plain VMAF rewards; higher is closer. PSNR, SSIM and MS-SSIM measure the pixels' and the structure's distance: higher is closer. FLIP is NVIDIA's measure of what a viewer sees when flipping between two pictures: lower is closer. Each takes the native frame as its reference (below ×1, the Lanczos-3 reduction), from your GPU's own pictures, so a score can differ a little from the README's, read on SwiftShader. These measures are made for SDR: the HDR pictures get the overshoot alone. MWSR follows LPIPS first, then VMAF NEG among the settings within 5 % of the best LPIPS. This page's inputs are reduced, never encoded; MoonlightWeb tuned MWSR on pictures that went through a video encoder at a stream's bit rate, where the order between the upscalers can differ.
  • Overshoot is the share of pixels that leave the range of their four input texels by more than 2/255 (on HDR, in BT.2020 nits, past 1 % + 0.5 nit). It counts halos and ringing.
  • GPU time is the median of 3 rounds of 6 batches of 100 draws, timed by GPU timestamps. It runs when you press the button, after the scores. While this page draws or scrolls the table waits, and a batch it touched is thrown away and taken again. The page sees only its own use of the GPU: what another tab or program asks of it stays in these figures. A slow draw splits its batch into pieces of about 100 ms of GPU, so that a change of menu never waits long. The browser rounds the timestamps (to 0.1 ms in Chrome), and a batch of 100 makes the rounding a small share. When your GPU has shader-f16, FSR1 and SGSR1 are also timed in half precision (16-bit) and the faster of their two precisions is shown, with Half precision under the name when it is that one. Within 2 % of each other the two are even, Half ≈ full: such a gap changes sides from one run to the next. MWSR is shown in 32-bit, the text MoonlightWeb runs; its enlargement's 16-bit text is timed too, and that time only goes into what measuring sends. Each 16-bit picture is first held to its 32-bit one, within 4/255 on 99.9 % of the pixels, or the row says Half refused and gives its 32-bit time. The pictures and the scores are those of the 32-bit texts. Timings vary with the GPU's clock: run them twice before you trust a small gap.
  • 4:2:0 gives every upscaler the input a 4:2:0 stream would give it: its colour averaged over 2×2 pixels and brought back by interpolation, as a decoder hands it over. This page makes both inputs itself, so every GPU shows them. A 4:4:4 stream needs more: a host whose encoder makes it (on MoonlightWeb's own host, NVENC in H.264 or HEVC) and a browser that decodes it. MoonlightWeb streams it in 8-bit SDR and offers it only where the browser says it decodes it.
  • MWSR sharpens colour edges too runs CHROMA444, the mode MoonlightWeb turns on by itself for a 4:4:4 stream: MWSR then also sharpens the border between two colours of one brightness, as in coloured text or code. Zoom on the colour text to see it. It does nothing on 4:2:0, whose colours are already blurred, and it is off by default, as in the README's figures.
  • HDR: the pictures are the lab's, drawn by this page with 1.0 = 203 nits and highlights up to 1000 nits. MWSR and bilinear read the floats past 1.0 as they are; MWSR then takes no margin on its bound, and its reduction does not sharpen. FSR1 runs inside AMD's reversible tone map (FsrSrtm), as AMD prescribes. NIS and SGSR1 run inside the same tone map, because neither port reads past 1.0 (MoonlightWeb's NIS port has no NIS_HDR_MODE, and SGSR1 clamps to 0..1). The tone map is not timed.
  • Your picture is decoded in this tab, and a picture larger than 3840×2160 is first reduced to fit. As the native frame, it is reduced and rebuilt, and scored (below ×1, it is the input, scored against its own Lanczos-3 reduction). As the input, it is upscaled as it is and only the overshoot is scored. Pictures are read as 8-bit SDR.

The ports

These are MoonlightWeb's ports, the ones the client runs, not the vendors' own builds. FSR1 is firdawolf's WGSL port of EASU and RCAS (RCAS at 0.75 stops), with the lab's port of AMD's 16-bit path (FsrEasuH, FsrRcasH). NIS is a per-fragment transcription of NVScaler (SDK 1.0.3) at sharpness 0.5. NVIDIA ships a compute shader with shared memory, which is faster, so its time here is the port's, not NIS's. SGSR1 is a port of sgsr1.h (mode 1, edge threshold 6/255, edge sharpness 1.5), in 32-bit and in half precision. MWSR runs with its defaults.

Outside the browser

This page times the browser's texts of the shaders, WGSL and GLSL through WebGPU or WebGL2. The texts in mwsr/ for Direct3D 12, Vulkan and Metal draw the same picture, but in a native renderer their time differs: another compiler, no browser in between, and the renderer's own passes around them. First readings in Moonlight-qt's renderers, Junk Shop from 1080p and 720p to 1440p, by GPU timestamps (06/11/2026): on an RTX 5060 Ti in Direct3D 12, MWSR takes 0.06 ms a frame and FSR1 0.23 ms, where this page's WebGPU gives about 0.08 and 0.3 ms; on a Radeon 780M in Moonlight-qt's Vulkan renderer (libplacebo, Linux), frames drawn back to back, MWSR runs as one pass that reads the decoder's planes and writes the output, and the whole frame costs 0.93 ms from 1080p and 0.86 ms from 720p, against 1.18 and 1.15 ms with FSR1, and 1.18 and 1.01 ms with the hook in mwsr/, around which libplacebo converts the colours. One picture and one run per GPU: a first reading, not a table. In a real stream on a 14-inch MacBook Pro (M1 Pro, macOS 15.6, steady 60 fps, no frame lost), Moonlight-qt's Vulkan path draws MWSR in a single pass straight from the decoder's planes: about 3.0 ms of GPU time per frame at ×2, against 5.3 to 6.7 ms for FSR1 — half the cost, as on the Radeon 780M at 60 Hz, 1.32 against 2.49 ms — and 3.2 against 4.5 ms at ×3.
